A story about the Controller Area Network

The Night the Brakes Had to Talk

Six acts, four computers, one shared pair of wires, and a promise that took 13 years to get right.

[Presenter] · [Course] · [Date] · ← → to move · N notes · F fullscreen

Lights low. “Tonight I’m going to tell you a story. It happens inside a car, in about a quarter of a millisecond. It has heroes, a villain, a race, and a twist that fooled experts for 13 years. And every rule in it is real.” The rainy-night scenario is fictional; every protocol fact is from the Bosch specification, ISO 11898 or peer-reviewed papers.

Prologue · Stuttgart, 1983

Once upon a time, every wire had one job, and cars were drowning in copper.

Each sensor needed its own wire to every computer that wanted it. Engineers at Robert Bosch dreamed of something else: one shared line that everyone could talk on.

1983

Bosch begins developing CAN

1986

Revealed at the SAE Congress

1991

First series car: the Mercedes S-Class. Bosch publishes CAN 2.0

1993

Becomes the international standard ISO 11898

Davis et al., Real-Time Systems 35(3), 2007, §1.1 · Bosch CAN Specification 2.0 (1991)

Set the scene. Before CAN, the wiring harness grew with every new feature: point-to-point wiring, one wire per connection. Bosch’s idea was a single shared bus. Four dates: 1983 the work starts, 1986 the public reveal, 1991 the first production car, 1993 the ISO standard.

The street they share is the CAN bus: one twisted pair of wires. The IDs are made up for this story, but the rules are real.

Introduce the characters like a film trailer. Bria the brakes has the lowest ID, which will matter in Act III. Rusty is our villain-by-accident. Keep the IDs on the board; students will meet them again in the arbitration race.

Rule №1 of the street

Nobody says who a message is for. They only say what it is, and the label is also its rank.

Bria never says “Engie, listen.” She says “BRAKE PRESSURE.” Everyone hears it, and each computer’s acceptance filter decides whether to keep it. A lower ID number means a higher priority.

Bosch CAN Specification 2.0 (1991), Part B, “Basic Concepts”

The most important idea of the whole talk. No destination addresses: content-based identifiers. Adding a new computer doesn’t require changing anyone else. And because the identifier is also the priority, the race in Act III resolves itself.

Rule №2 · Shouting beats whispering

On this street, “0” is a shout and “1” is silence

BitCAN_HCAN_LDifference
1 · recessive (silence)≈ 2.5 V≈ 2.5 V≈ 0 V (< 0.5 V)
0 · dominant (shout)≈ 3.5 V≈ 1.5 V≈ 2 V (> 0.9 V)

If even one computer shouts, the whole street hears a shout. Engineers call it wired-AND.

In the model, rain and engine noise shake both wires together, but the green difference never moves. Receivers only listen to the difference.

noise moves both wires · difference unchanged

CiA, “CAN HS transmission” · ISO 11898-2 · Bosch CAN Spec. 2.0, “Bus Values”

Recessive: nobody drives, both lines near 2.5 V. Dominant: CAN_H to about 3.5 V and CAN_L to about 1.5 V. One dominant driver overrides all recessive ones, and that is the secret weapon for Act III. The twisted pair cancels noise because noise hits both wires equally.

Rule №3 · Sponges at both ends

One street, a 120 Ω resistor at each end

every computer hangs off the same line · signals travel both ways

Without the end resistors, signals would bounce back like waves hitting a wall. The resistors soak them up, matching the cable’s 120 Ω impedance, and pull the street back to silence (recessive) when nobody talks.

ISO 11898-2 · CiA, “CAN HS transmission” · TI SLOA101B

Ask: why not put the resistor inside each computer? Because if that computer is unplugged, the street loses its sponge. ISO 11898 puts them at the two ends of the line.
II

Act II

The rainy night

23:47:12 · wet road · 50 km/h

A ball bounces into the road. The driver stamps on the pedal. In the very same instant, Bria, Engie and Dash all start talking.

Pause here. Lower your voice. “Three computers start talking at exactly the same time, on the same two wires. On most networks, that’s a collision: garbage. What happens on CAN?” Let students guess before the next slide. The scenario is fictional; the race that follows is exact.

Act II · The race

They all speak, but only the most urgent finishes

SOF · ID10 → ID0 · RTR

Speak and listen

Each computer sends its ID one bit at a time while listening to the street. Whoever stays silent (1) but hears a shout (0) knows someone more urgent is talking, and drops out instantly.

How it plays out

Dash (0x123) drops at bit ID8. Engie (0x067) holds on until ID1. Bria (0x065) never even notices the race.

Nothing is lost

Bria’s message arrives intact, and no bus time is wasted. The losers retry automatically when the street is quiet.

Bosch CAN Specification 2.0 (1991), Part B, “Basic Concepts” and “Message Transfer” (bitwise arbitration)

Run it live with three volunteers holding ID cards: everyone calls out their bits together, and anyone who says 1 while someone says 0 sits down. Tall glowing cubes are shouts (0); low dark cubes are silence (1). The amber cube is the moment someone realises they lost. Lowest ID wins: that is why brakes get small IDs.

Act II · What Bria actually said

Every message is a tiny, perfectly formatted letter

Envelope

Start of Frame wakes everyone. The 11-bit ID names the content. RTR marks data or a request.

Pages

IDE and r0, then DLC: 0–8 bytes of data, sent MSB first.

Seal

15-bit CRC. ACK: any receiver that got it right overwrites one bit. EOF: 7 recessive bits.

Bosch CAN Specification 2.0 (1991), Part B, “Message Transfer” · block widths follow bit counts; stuff bits not shown

The letter metaphor: SOF is the knock on the door, the ID is the title on the envelope, DLC is the page count, the CRC is a wax seal that proves nothing was smudged, and the ACK is a friend shouting “got it!”. Fun fact: the sender leaves the ACK slot silent; receivers shout into it.
III

Act III

The villain

23:47:13 · Rusty starts transmitting

Rain has crept into Rusty’s corroded connector. Every time it talks, some of its bits come out flipped. Could one broken computer drown out the whole car?

Every story needs a villain, and Rusty isn’t evil, just broken. The question for the room: “If one node keeps sending garbage, can it bring down the network?” In many designs the answer would be yes. In CAN, the network has an immune system.

Act III · Five detectives on every wire

Garbage never gets past the guards

Frame bits: 0 0 0 0 0 0 0 1 On the bus: 0 0 0 0 0 1 0 0 1 ← stuff bit

Bit stuffing: after five identical bits the sender adds one opposite bit, and receivers remove it. This keeps every clock in step, and makes six identical bits in a row an alarm.

Residual risk of an undetected corrupted message: below message error rate × 4.7·10⁻¹¹.

DetectiveCatches …
BitA sender reading back a different bit than it sent (outside arbitration and the ACK slot)
StuffSix identical bits in a row
CRCA checksum that doesn’t match
FormA fixed bit with the wrong value
ACKNobody confirming they received it

Bosch CAN Specification 2.0 (1991), Part B, “Bit Stream Coding” and “Error Handling”

Rusty’s flipped bits are caught at once. The first computer to notice shouts an error flag of six dominant bits, which deliberately breaks the stuffing rule so everyone notices. The frame is thrown away for everyone and retransmitted. But if Rusty keeps failing, the retransmissions would clog the street. Next slide.

Act III · Yellow card, red card

Rusty sends itself off the pitch

Rusty’s TEC = 0
StateRule
Error-activeTEC ≤ 127 and REC ≤ 127. Can shout error flags (6 dominant bits)
Error-passiveTEC or REC ≥ 128. Only whispers error flags (6 recessive bits) and waits 8 extra bits after transmitting
Bus-offTEC ≥ 256. Leaves the bus; may return after 128 × 11 recessive bits

Each transmit error costs +8; each success earns back only −1. After 32 straight failures, Rusty is out, and Bria, Engie and Dash keep talking.

Bosch CAN Specification 2.0 (1991), Part B, “Fault Confinement”

The football analogy: fouls cost 8 points and good play earns back 1. At 128, yellow card: you can play, but quietly. At 256, red card: you leave so the game continues. Rusty disconnects itself. No central referee is needed; each node judges itself. The car’s software can notice Rusty’s missing messages and warn the driver, which is an application design choice on top of CAN.
IV

Act IV

The promise

the next morning · an engineer’s desk

The car stopped in time. But an engineer asks the real question: not “did it work last night?” but “can I prove Bria’s message will always arrive before its deadline?”

The turn from story to science. Testing shows that something worked once. Real-time engineering demands a guarantee for the worst case. This act gives students the tools: timing inside a bit, then the worst-case length of a frame, then the response-time equations.

Act IV · Everyone dances to one beat

Inside a single bit: 16 time quanta

SYNC 1 · PROP 7 · PHASE_SEG1 6 · PHASE_SEG2 2 → sample point 87.5 %

Hard sync

The first edge (SOF) restarts everyone’s bit clock.

Resync

Later edges nudge PHASE_SEG1/2 by up to SJW (1–4 TQ).

Distance limit

PROP_SEG covers the round-trip delay. Longer bus, slower bits.

Bosch CAN Spec. 2.0, Part B, “Bit Timing Requirements” · ISO 11898-1 (Phase_Seg2 set on its own, minimum range 2–8 TQ)

The race in Act II only works if every computer agrees on every bit at the same moment, like dancers to one drumbeat. The laser is the clock; the flash is the sample point. Bosch 1991 tied PHASE_SEG2 to PHASE_SEG1; ISO 11898-1 lets it be set on its own, which allows late sample points like 87.5 %.

Act IV · Measuring the longest letter

How long can one message possibly take?

Cm = ( g + 8·sm + 13 + ⌊(g + 8·sm − 1) / 4⌋ ) · τbit g = 34 (11-bit ID) · 54 (29-bit ID)

135

bits: worst-case 8-byte frame with an 11-bit ID, counting stuff bits and the 3-bit intermission

160

bits: the same frame with a 29-bit ID

270 µs

to send 135 bits at 500 kbit/s (2 µs per bit)

CAN behaves like fixed-priority, non-preemptive scheduling: the ID is the priority, and a message that has started cannot be interrupted.

Davis, Burns, Bril, Lukkien, Real-Time Systems 35(3):239–272, 2007, Eqs. 1–2

Do the arithmetic live: 34 + 64 + 13 + 24 = 135. The floor term is the worst possible number of stuff bits. Non-preemptive matters: even Bria may have to wait for one lower-priority message that is already on the wire.

Act IV · The engineer’s recipe

Three kinds of waiting add up to the worst case

Blocking

One lower-priority message already on the wire.

Interference

Higher-priority messages that win the race first.

Busy period

Check every copy of the message while the bus stays busy.

Davis et al. 2007, Eqs. 5, 8, 10, 11, 13 (T = period, J = queuing jitter, hp/hep = higher / higher-or-equal priority)

Solve each line by fixed-point iteration: start small and repeat until it stops changing. Keep the last card in mind, “check every copy”. It is the key to the twist.
V

Act V

The twist

1994 → 2007 · University of York & TU Eindhoven

In 1994, Tindell, Burns and Wellings published the first way to compute CAN response times. It was elegant and widely adopted. Thirteen years later, Davis, Burns, Bril and Lukkien found a case it got wrong.

The plot twist. A trusted, widely used result, built into commercial tools used to design production cars, was optimistic. Ask: “How could a proof be wrong for 13 years?” Then show them.

Act V · The case that broke the proof

The first copy was fine. The second one wasn’t.

A, B and C each take 1 ms. A repeats every 2.5 ms. B and C repeat every 3.5 ms, with a 3.25 ms deadline. All three start together at t = 0.

3.0 ms

1994 analysis (first copy only): looks safe

3.5 ms

Real worst case: C’s second copy, released at 3.5, finishes at 7.0

> 3.25

Deadline missed

Davis, Burns, Bril, Lukkien, Real-Time Systems 35(3):239–272, 2007 (doi:10.1007/s11241-007-9012-7)

Watch the timeline build. C’s first copy finishes at 3 ms, safely. But its second copy arrives at 3.5 while the bus is still busy. A and B go first, and C ends at 7.0: 3.5 ms of response against a 3.25 ms deadline. The old method only checked the first copy.

Resolution

Science didn’t break. It corrected itself.

Revised analysis

Check every instance inside the busy period. That is the t, Q and q in the recipe.

Better priorities

Deadline-monotonic ordering isn’t optimal for CAN. Audsley’s algorithm (OPA) is.

The moral

“It worked in testing” and even “it was proven” both deserve a second look.

Davis et al. 2007 · Audsley, Information Processing Letters 79(1), 2001

Land the moral. The paper also recommended revising commercial analysis tools. This is how engineering is supposed to work: publish, scrutinise, correct.
